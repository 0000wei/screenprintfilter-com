Best Halftone Dot Size for Different Mesh Counts

Published: July 15, 2026 • 7 min read

Getting halftone dots right in screen printing comes down to one critical relationship: the size of your dots versus the mesh count of your screen. Use dots that are too large and you lose detail. Use dots that are too small and the ink bleeds through the mesh, ruining your print. Matching the two is the difference between a professional result and a frustrating waste of screens and ink.

This guide breaks down exactly which halftone dot sizes work with each common mesh count, how to test your setup, and the mistakes to avoid.

TL;DR: Higher mesh counts allow smaller, finer halftone dots. A 110 mesh screen needs 8-12px dots, while a 355 mesh can handle dots as small as 1-3px. Use our free online halftone tool to generate precisely sized dots for your specific mesh screen.

What is Mesh Count?

Mesh count refers to the number of threads per linear inch in a screen printing mesh. A 110 mesh screen has 110 threads per inch, while a 305 mesh screen has 305 threads per inch. The higher the mesh count, the finer the mesh openings, and the more detail the screen can hold.

Different mesh counts serve different purposes:

  • Low mesh (40-86): Heavy ink deposits for glitter, puff, or thick specialty inks. Not suitable for halftones.
  • Medium mesh (110-156): Standard textile printing. Works well for bold designs and basic halftones on dark fabrics.
  • High mesh (200-230): Detailed designs, light-colored fabrics, and fine halftone work.
  • Ultra-high mesh (305-355): CMYK process printing, fine art reproductions, and ultra-fine halftone dots.

When printing halftones, the mesh openings must be large enough for the smallest dot to pass through cleanly. If a dot is smaller than the mesh opening, it can fall through or distort — leading to muddy, bleeding edges.

Key Rule: As a general guideline, your smallest halftone dot should be at least 2-3 times larger than the mesh opening width. This ensures the dot sits securely on the mesh threads and transfers cleanly to the substrate.

Dot Size by Mesh Count

The table below shows the recommended halftone dot size range (in pixels at 300 DPI) for each common screen printing mesh count. These are starting points — your final dot size may vary depending on ink viscosity, squeegee pressure, and substrate texture.

110 Mesh 8 - 12 px Thick fabrics, dark inks, bold halftones on hoodies and heavy cotton
156 Mesh 6 - 9 px All-purpose standard prints, mid-tone halftones, general textile work
200 Mesh 4 - 7 px Detailed designs, light fabrics, fine halftones with good ink flow

355 Mesh 1 - 3 px Ultra-fine detail, fine art reproductions, museum-quality giclée style

How to Read the Chart

If you are printing on dark t-shirts with a 110 mesh screen, use halftone dots between 8 and 12 pixels at 300 DPI. The coarser mesh allows more ink to pass through, so you need larger dots to maintain shape and prevent bleeding. As you move to finer meshes, the dots shrink proportionally.

For CMYK process printing, a 305 mesh is the industry standard. Each color channel (cyan, magenta, yellow, black) gets its own halftone screen with dots at 2-4 pixels at the appropriate angle to avoid moiré patterns.

How to Test Your Dot Size

Before committing to a full production run, always run a step test. Here is a reliable method:

  1. Create a test strip with halftone dots ranging from 1 px to 15 px in 1 px increments, arranged side by side.
  2. Burn and expose the test strip onto your screen using your normal exposure time.
  3. Wash out the screen and inspect it under good light. Look for the smallest dot that fully washed out without any distortion.
  4. Print a test using your actual ink and substrate. Check for sharp edges, bleeding, and dot gain.
  5. Compare under a loupe — a 10x or 20x magnifier reveals exactly how each dot size performed on press.

Document the results for each mesh count and ink combination you use. Over time, you will build a personalised reference chart that accounts for your specific emulsion, exposure unit, and press setup.

Pro Tip: Many printers find that the actual usable dot size is 1-2 pixels larger than what the theoretical chart suggests, because of dot gain during exposure and ink spread on press. Always start at the larger end of the recommended range and work smaller.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Even experienced printers make these errors. Here is what to watch out for:

  • Dot too small for the mesh: The most frequent mistake. A 2 px dot on a 110 mesh screen will fall through the mesh openings, causing ink bleeding, fuzzy edges, and a ruined print. Always check the mesh-to-dot ratio first.
  • Using the same dot size for all mesh counts: Each mesh count demands a different dot range. What works beautifully on a 230 mesh will plug up or bleed on a 110 mesh.
  • Ignoring ink viscosity: Thicker inks (white, opaque, specialty) need larger dots regardless of mesh count. Thin, transparent inks can hold finer dots.
  • Over-exposing the screen: Excessive exposure time hardens the emulsion beyond the intended dot boundary, shrinking your halftone dots or closing them entirely. Fine-tune exposure with a step wedge.
  • Not accounting for dot gain: A 4 px dot in your digital file may print as a 6 px dot on fabric due to mechanical dot gain. Compensate by designing slightly smaller than your target.

From Theory to Practice

Every print shop is different. Your ink brand, emulsion type, exposure unit, squeegee durometer, and even the humidity in your shop will shift your optimal dot size. Use the chart in this guide as your starting point, then dial in through testing.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I use 110 mesh for halftone printing?

Yes, but only with larger dots (8-12 px). 110 mesh is great for bold halftone effects on dark or textured fabrics where you need heavy ink coverage. It is not suitable for fine photographic detail.

What is the best all-purpose mesh for halftones?

156 mesh is the most versatile. It handles dot sizes from 6 to 9 pixels comfortably, works with most ink types, and produces clean results on both light and dark fabrics. Many shops keep 156 as their go-to standard.

Do I need a different mesh for CMYK halftone printing?

Yes. CMYK process printing typically requires a 305 mesh screen for each color channel. The fine mesh openings (around 48 microns) allow the small 2-4 px dots to reproduce smooth photographic tones without moiré interference when the screens are angled correctly.

How do I convert dot size from pixels to microns?

At 300 DPI, one pixel equals approximately 85 microns. So a 4 px halftone dot is roughly 340 microns. Compare this to your mesh opening size: for a 305 mesh (48 micron openings), a 340 micron dot is about 7 times larger than the opening — well within the safe range.
